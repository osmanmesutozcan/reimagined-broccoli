# Crafting an experience

Start with a shared understanding of the person and their problem. Use the laws to find useful questions, then shape an experience whose choices we can explain and test.

## The soul

People bring expectations, limited attention, and a purpose. Help them recognize where they are, understand their choices, act with confidence, and see what happened. Let the system carry avoidable effort. Give necessary effort meaning, support, and a clear end.

This is our synthesis of Laws of UX, not a universal formula. Its collection mixes research findings, cognitive concepts, and design heuristics. Treat each as a lens: applicability depends on the person, task, and situation. User evidence can outweigh a plausible law-based explanation; accessibility and honest communication remain design requirements.

## 1. Explore together

Read the brief and inspect the existing experience or supplied artifacts. Establish:

- **Person and moment:** who is using this, what prompted them, what they know, and their device, access needs, or situational constraints.
- **Outcome:** what they want to accomplish and how they should feel when finished.
- **Journey:** where they enter, what they must understand or decide, where they struggle, and what happens afterward.
- **Boundaries:** product constraints, consequential mistakes, and the observable evidence that would count as improvement.

Separate observations from assumptions. Ask the human partner the smallest question that could change the direction; use a concrete scenario or sketch when words leave competing interpretations. Reuse answers already supplied.

**Done when:** state the person, situation, desired outcome, main friction, and constraints in a short shared brief. Resolve disagreements or direction-changing unknowns with the human before choosing a solution. Carry minor unknowns as explicit assumptions that can be tested.

## 2. Choose the lenses

Use the reference map below to read only the laws that could change a decision. Begin with the friction in the brief. For each selected law, connect **observed friction → relevant mechanism → possible change → tradeoff → observable check**. With no user evidence, label the friction and mechanism as hypotheses.

**Done when:** every selected lens explains a specific part of the journey, and the reasoning names where it might fail. Several laws describing the same mechanism add no independent evidence. Leave irrelevant laws unused.

## 3. Shape the experience

Propose a direction grounded in the shared brief. Show a meaningful alternative when a tradeoff remains open. Make the journey concrete through copy, a walkthrough, or a prototype: entry, action, feedback, completion, and relevant failure or return paths.

When laws pull in different directions, compare the consequences for this person. For example, revealing fewer fields may ease initial choice but force repeated navigation for an expert. Prefer the option that serves the agreed outcome; name the cost it accepts and what evidence would reverse the choice.

**Done when:** the human can understand what the user will experience, why the key choices fit, and which uncertainty needs checking. Incorporate their corrections before committing to the direction; existing clear direction needs no repeat approval.

## 4. Check and refine

Use the smallest check that tests the uncertain claim: a task walkthrough, prototype session, accessibility check, or comparison with observed behavior. Watch for hesitation, mistakes, recovery, and understanding of the result. Ask about confidence when it matters to the brief.

**Done when:** report the proposed or revised experience, decisive rationale, evidence collected, and remaining uncertainty. Distinguish a design inspection from a test with users. If testing is unavailable, supply a concrete validation plan and keep the outcome provisional.

## Reference map

Each reference contains the core idea, our application guidance, a limit, and a check. Coverage follows the 30 entries on Laws of UX reviewed on 2026-09-15. Source links live with each law; examples and checks are our interpretations.

### Understanding and learning

- When behavior violates familiar conventions: [Jakob’s Law](references/jakobs-law.md).
- When users predict the wrong result or misunderstand the system: [Mental Model](references/mental-model.md).
- When people begin acting before learning the tool: [Paradox of the Active User](references/paradox-of-the-active-user.md).
- When framing or our own assumptions may distort a decision: [Cognitive Bias](references/cognitive-bias.md).

### Effort and decisions

- When a task feels mentally demanding: [Cognitive Load](references/cognitive-load.md).
- When information must be remembered across steps: [Working Memory](references/working-memory.md).
- When memory capacity is used to justify an item limit: [Miller’s Law](references/millers-law.md).
- When information needs meaningful groups: [Chunking](references/chunking.md).
- When choosing the next action takes too long: [Hick’s Law](references/hicks-law.md).
- When comparing options causes indecision or abandonment: [Choice Overload](references/choice-overload.md).
- When deciding who should handle unavoidable complexity: [Tesler’s Law](references/teslers-law.md).
- When comparing solutions with unnecessary elements or assumptions: [Occam’s Razor](references/occams-razor.md).
- When prioritizing which friction to address: [Pareto Principle](references/pareto-principle.md).
- When a bounded task expands into unnecessary work: [Parkinson’s Law](references/parkinsons-law.md).

### Perception and attention

- When spacing suggests the wrong relationships: [Law of Proximity](references/law-of-proximity.md).
- When a boundary should establish a group: [Law of Common Region](references/law-of-common-region.md).
- When matching appearances imply matching roles: [Law of Similarity](references/law-of-similarity.md).
- When explicit links must communicate relationships: [Law of Uniform Connectedness](references/law-of-uniform-connectedness.md).
- When a visual form is difficult to interpret: [Law of Prägnanz](references/law-of-pragnanz.md).
- When people overlook task-relevant information or changes: [Selective Attention](references/selective-attention.md).
- When one item needs to stand out: [Von Restorff Effect](references/von-restorff-effect.md).
- When polish influences perceived ease or trust: [Aesthetic-Usability Effect](references/aesthetic-usability-effect.md).

### Action and momentum

- When controls are difficult to reach or select: [Fitts’s Law](references/fittss-law.md).
- When response delays interrupt interaction: [Doherty Threshold](references/doherty-threshold.md).
- When sustained work loses rhythm or control: [Flow](references/flow.md).
- When input formats create avoidable rejection: [Postel’s Law](references/postels-law.md).
- When distance to completion affects motivation: [Goal-Gradient Effect](references/goal-gradient-effect.md).

### Memory and closure

- When interrupted work needs a clear return path: [Zeigarnik Effect](references/zeigarnik-effect.md).
- When position in a sequence affects recall: [Serial Position Effect](references/serial-position-effect.md).
- When emotional high points or endings shape the remembered journey: [Peak-End Rule](references/peak-end-rule.md).
